Do sub-arctic soil fungal communities adapt in response to chronic warming?

An assessment of thermal adaptation in soil fungi after five years of experimental warming near Abisko, at both the community and the isolate level.

Background

Background and aims

The Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the global mean, and its soils store large quantities of organic carbon. Fungi are major decomposers of this carbon, so whether their physiology adjusts to sustained warming is important for predicting soil carbon loss.

Community-level assays of bacteria have shown upward shifts in temperature optima under warming, but it remains unclear whether this reflects adaptation of the organisms or turnover in community composition, and the equivalent test had not been made for fungi at the level of individual isolates. The study addressed four questions at two levels of organisation:

  1. Does chronic warming change fungal diversity, composition or abundance?
  2. Do the thermal traits of the whole soil community shift?
  3. Do the thermal traits of individual cultured isolates shift?
  4. Are there trade-offs among thermal traits?
Study site

Experimental warming at Abisko

In a mountain-birch forest near Abisko, northern Sweden, eight 1 m² plots of forest floor have been part of a warming experiment since 2020. Half of the plots are heated year-round with infrared heaters.

Soil temperature loggers recorded a realised warming of +1.32 °C, reaching approximately +2 °C in summer, comparable to the warming projected for these soils in the coming decades. I took part in repeated sampling campaigns at the site, collecting topsoil from four points per plot.

Methods

Three independent measurements

01 · Community composition

Amplicon sequencing and qPCR

ITS2 and 16S amplicon sequencing characterised fungal and bacterial community composition; 18S qPCR and ergosterol quantified fungal biomass.

02 · Community growth

Whole-soil temperature response

Growth of the resident fungal community from 0 to 45 °C, measured as ¹⁴C-acetate incorporation into ergosterol and quantified by HPLC and liquid scintillation counting.

03 · Isolate physiology

141 isolates in pure culture

Fungi were isolated by dilution plating from warmed and control soil and grown at nine temperatures, so that the minimum, optimum and maximum growth temperatures of each strain could be measured directly.

Analysis

Thermal performance modelling

For each isolate I fitted the cardinal temperature model with inflection (CTMI). It yields the minimum, optimum and maximum growth temperatures and the peak growth rate: a complete thermal profile for each isolate rather than a community average.

I built the analysis pipeline in R: mixed models matched to the blocked design, exact restricted-permutation PERMANOVA, ordination and nonlinear curve fitting with residual diagnostics. Every fit was screened for whether its thermal limits were really identified by the data, or only pinned to the outermost temperature measured.

Laboratory work

Isolation, culture and measurement

Results

No detectable thermal adaptation

Warmed and control soils were indistinguishable: fungal community composition, fungal biomass and the community temperature response did not differ. Isolates from warmed plots showed the same thermal range, the same optimum temperature and the same survival after exposure to 35 °C.

The optimum temperature shifted by +0.26 °C, consistent with the shift expected from the realised warming (+0.25 to +0.38 °C) and small relative to the 3.8 °C variation among isolates. Growth rates of warmed isolates were, if anything, slightly lower, contrary to the expectation under heat adaptation. Thermal traits were positively correlated rather than showing trade-offs.

Variation among isolates

Independent of the warming treatment, isolates that recovered after high-temperature exposure had higher optimum and maximum growth temperatures and approximately half the thermal trait width of those that did not. Substantial thermal variation therefore exists among isolates and is associated with acute heat survival.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Five years of chronic +1.32 °C warming produced no detectable thermal adaptation. On this evidence, sub-arctic soil fungi are resistant to modest warming rather than adjusting to it.

The three measurement approaches gave consistent results. Particular attention was given to establishing the detection limits of the design, so that the negative result can be interpreted with confidence.
